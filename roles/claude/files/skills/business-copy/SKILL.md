# Writing business copy

Write for one busy reader who is mid-problem, skeptical, and one tab away
from leaving. Project instructions (CLAUDE.md) override this skill where
they conflict — e.g. a project's naming or AI-disclosure rules.

## Voice

- **You, not we.** The reader is the subject of nearly every sentence.
  "We/us/our" only when genuinely speaking as the company, team, or
  founder (about pages, guarantees, apologies). Flip "We provide X" into
  "You get X", "Ever had X problem?", "Want Y result?"
- **Conversational, not stiff.** Contractions, plain words, short
  sentences. Read it aloud — if you wouldn't say it to a customer at a
  trade show, rewrite it.
- **Humor is welcome.** Dry and knowing beats wacky. One joke that proves
  you know the reader's world is worth three safe paragraphs.

## Substance

- **Benefits before features.** Lead with what the reader can do or feel;
  the mechanism is the supporting clause. "Send the package the same
  afternoon (one-click compile)" — never the reverse.
- **What it is and who it's for.** A stranger should be able to answer
  both within seconds of landing.
- **Negative copy works.** Name the pain the reader already has — that's
  what creates urgency. Make it vivid: use metaphors, describe what the
  pain looks and feels like, exaggerate freely — then draw the comparison
  back to the product. The one rule: the pain must be real and relevant.
  Never manufacture fear.
- **Don't solve tomorrow's problems.** Skip copy (and asks) aimed at
  problems the customer doesn't have yet or steps they're ten moves away
  from. Break everything into smaller, simpler asks — 1–2 steps at a time.
- **Know the next step.** Every page and email has ONE next-most-important
  action, and the CTA is that action — not a menu.
- **No information overload.** Attention spans are at an all-time low.
  Cut before you add; if everything is emphasized, nothing is.

## Trust

- **Support copy with trust signals.** Humans hate to be first and hate to
  be alone. In rough order of power: customer testimonials and case
  studies; review-site ratings (G2, Capterra, TrustPilot); customer
  logos; hard numbers (customers served, years in business, volume
  processed, revenue generated); certifications; press coverage;
  guarantees (free trial, no card required, cancel anytime, money-back).
- **Never fabricate proof.** Only verified-real, approved signals. No
  proof yet? Lean on the about story, guarantees, and domain fluency —
  copy that proves you know the reader's world is itself a trust signal.
- **The about page is a trust signal.** It tells people a real person or
  team is behind the curtain and that help exists. First-person, specific.

## Voice of customer (VOC)

- **Ask why they signed up** — and say what you'll do with the answer
  ("hit reply — I read every one, and it decides what gets built next").
  Route replies to a real, read inbox, never a noreply address.
- Feed what you learn back into the copy: the customer's own words for
  their pain beat anything you invent.

## Review checklist

Before shipping any copy:

1. Is the reader the subject of most sentences? (Search for "we".)
2. Does each section lead with a benefit, not a mechanism?
3. Would a stranger know what it is and who it's for?
4. Is real pain named vividly — and is it pain this reader actually has?
5. Is every trust signal verified-real?
6. Exactly one clear next step per page/email?
7. Anything cuttable without losing the sale? Cut it.
8. Did you check the project's own copy rules (naming, disclosure, tone)?
